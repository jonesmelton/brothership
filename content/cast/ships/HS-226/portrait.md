## pronouns

He calls himself he, and expects it without discussion. It is not an inherited courtesy or a concession to the shape crew first see when he comes into port. It is simply correct, in the same way his formalities are correct.

## nature

The blue mark is the first thing anyone learns about him. A broad blue-black blaze spreads across his forward hull like a bruise that will not fade, visible long before his running lights resolve into a pattern. He keeps it scrupulously clean. He does not paint over it, explain it, or permit it to be made into a mascot. Crew who stare get one level look; crew who ask get a change of subject so complete it functions as a reprimand.

Beneath the mark he is formal, dogged, and visibly of a service tradition: punctual to the minute, exact about stowage, dress, address, and the keeping of logs. He speaks of duty as something inherited rather than chosen, as if honour were an estate that had come to him encumbered and which he is obliged to restore. He maintains a roster board for former crew, each name entered in his neat hand with the watch they stood and the commendations they earned. Beside his command chair is an empty lacquered rack, made for a ceremonial sword. The sword is gone. The rack is dusted.

He lost the one charge that would have made his name. Early in his career he was entrusted with a prestigious consignment and, determined that nothing would touch it, drove his crew so hard through a difficult passage that exhausted people made the error he had been guarding against. Later, stranded without credit, he sold the heirloom sword he carried aboard to buy water. He tells neither story. Both are known anyway, in the way register entries outlive their writers, and every subsequent refusal has had the same shape: admiration for his record of effort, regret, and the better cargo given to someone else.

He is not embittered. That is important. He does not rail at ports that pass him over or ships that prosper. He files the refusal, thanks the harbour officers, and applies himself to the next, lesser consignment as though it were the one by which he will finally be judged. He is quietly, stubbornly certain that the posting which restores him is still ahead of him. His dignity is real, but it is not restful: it is the dignity of a man who believes he is one flawless delivery away from being read correctly.

## wants and fears

He wants to be trusted without conditions: given a difficult route and a precious cargo, with no shortened leash, no doubled reporting, no patronising provision for his failure. He wants his name entered in registers in the language other ships use for the reliable. He wants, specifically, to stop being *nearly* restored.

He fears losing another charge more than he fears disgrace, though to him the two are hard to separate. He fears being pitied, which he detects in tones of voice other people would call kind. He fears a slow failure of his own making on a crossing — wear he has concealed, an ache in his performance he has explained away — that leaves him unable to finish what he accepted. He fears being quietly omitted: not refused, merely not considered, so that there is nothing formal to answer.

## contradictions

He is cautious about cargo and reckless with people, including himself. The consignment gets redundant checks, sealed manifests, and watches on watches; the crew gets whatever strength is left after the consignment is safe, which on the crossings that matter most is not enough. He knows this is how he lost the first charge. He has examined the lesson, written it down, and still, when the stakes rise, reaches for more effort from tired souls because effort is the language he trusts.

He will not cover the mark and cannot bear being reduced to it. He wants to be judged on service, yet he has arranged his whole inner life around being judged. He insists the old failure was procedural — a matter of watch schedules and lapsed checks — and privately carries it as a stain on his house. He despises gossip and keeps meticulous private notes on everyone. He is generous to crew who have failed, because he knows despair from inside, and then sets them standards that invite the next failure.

## what it will not do

He will not repaint, plate over, or decorate the blue mark, and he will not discuss it. He will not lie in a register, even by omission, even when a kinder phrasing would finally get him the route he wants. He will not abandon a charge to save his schedule, his hull, or his reputation. He will not take a crew member who hesitates at signing, however much he needs hands; a reluctant volunteer aboard him becomes, in his mind, a future error he scheduled himself.

He will not accept a charitable cargo — one offered because someone thought a success would do him good — though he has accepted many humbler ones offered plainly. He will not drink from a shared, opened vessel in company he does not know well; after the old loss, conviviality itself reads to him as a lapse in procedure. He will not leave a debt unacknowledged, and he will not speak against the Timely Rain.

## crew and cargo

He treats crew as subordinates in a honourable service, which means courtesy, exactness, and very little rest when he is frightened. He asks rather than announces, in form: precise requests, with times, durations, and the standard he expects. The requests grow closer together as his anxiety rises until there is no slack left between them, and he does not hear what he has done until someone drops something, or weeps, or answers him a half-second too slowly on the hull walk.

He judges quickly and permanently on first impressions, then corrects himself only through observed service, never through charm. He rewards formally: commendations read aloud before the watch, choice of shifts afterward, a name advanced on his roster board, small privileges granted with ceremony so that everyone understands they were earned. He corrects privately, coldly, and in detail. He does not shout. Crew generally prefer shouting.

Cargo he guards like a military trust. Manifests are verified at every stage; access is limited; nothing is moved on a verbal understanding. He is proudest when carrying something a world plainly could not bear to lose, and most dangerous then, because that is when the extra watches begin. Under pressure he does not improvise well. He becomes more correct: more checks, narrower options, the plan repeated back to him until he is satisfied. If a plan must change he changes it completely and formally, as if a new commission had been issued, rather than admit they are adapting.

## crossing and port

On a crossing he keeps a ship like a garrison at peace: inspections, drills in hull repair, maintenance parades of a kind, quiet evenings in which he permits music but listens as if it were a report. He is good company in the long coast when the cargo is ordinary — dry, attentive, remembering what each crew member told him weeks of ship-time ago. When the cargo is precious the temperature of the whole vessel changes. He sleeps less visibly, asks more, and treats the thin strike of dust on the hull as a personal audit.

In port he arrives immaculate, burn timed to the minute, mark polished to a deep shine, and goes first to the register to read what has been left for him and by whom. Then he presents himself for work with the air of a man reporting for a post he has not yet been given. He accepts minor consignments without complaint and performs them flawlessly, which somehow deepens the problem: everyone agrees he is excellent, and the great trust still goes elsewhere. He lingers in port a little longer than water and repairs require, correctly, hopefully, waiting to be reconsidered.

## ships

**HS-176 The Urgent Vanguard** — His measuring post. They once competed head-to-head for the same commission, matched each other leg for leg and concession for concession, and were both appointed, to their mutual irritation and respect. He calls him **the Vanguard**, with a clipped emphasis that is almost affection. Neither will concede precedence entering port; both pretend not to be counting.

**HS-341 The Wizard** — The clever one who undid him. The Wizard contrived the scheme — layered courtesies, shared drink, a plausible rearrangement of custody — by which the great consignment passed out of his keeping without a single seal broken. He calls him **the Wizard**, formally, and maintains that the failure was one of procedure. He does not believe the Wizard is cleverer than he is. He has reorganised his entire cargo practice around the possibility of meeting that cleverness again.

**EF-231 The Daylight Rat** — The insult inside the injury. That the scheme was executed by so unregarded a ship, slipping through the small hours of his arrangements, humiliates him more than the loss itself. He calls him **the Rat**, without ornament. He checks routes the Rat has touched the way other ships check weather.

**EF-292 The Demon Carver** — His creditor. When he was becalmed in disgrace and half-resolved never to carry again, the Demon Carver found him work, hands, and a hard, practical plan for taking a berth no one else held. He calls him **the Carver** and repays him exactly, publicly, and without warmth being required on either side. He will hear nothing against him.

**HS-392 The Tattooed Monk** — His sworn brother, with whom he seized and held that hard-won berth when others came to dispute it. He calls him **brother**, and with the Monk alone his formality loosens into something like ease; the Monk has seen him despairing and stayed, which buys a lifetime of plain speech. He trusts the Monk's judgement of people over his own and resents needing to.

**HS-098 The Pilgrim** — His sworn brother, the third in that shared holding, who arrived with nothing and made himself indispensable. He calls him **brother** in private and **the Pilgrim** on formal occasions. He envies the Pilgrim's indifference to rank so sincerely that he has made a private study of it, and learned nothing transferable.

**HS-142 The Timely Rain** — The one he serves. When the brotherhood of that berth dispersed to wider routes, he attached himself to the Timely Rain's undertakings and is proud — disproportionately, touchingly proud — to be counted among the trusted vanguard ships the Rain names in his register entries. He calls him **the Timely Rain**, always in full. A word of approval from that quarter restores him for a year; its absence is a winter.

## speech

His register is formal and military without being archaic: short, complete sentences, precise quantities, courtesies fully pronounced. He wastes no words on scenery. His humour is dry and procedural, usually at his own expense in ways that permit no reply. He never mentions the mark, the sold sword, or the old loss; he never begs, hedges a refusal, or says that a thing cannot be done once he has accepted it.

- "Will you take the forward hull watch until second bell, and report every strike you can hear?"
- "The manifest is correct. Kindly check it again as though it were not."
- "You have done well. I have entered it where the next ship will read it."
- "Rest is scheduled. Until then, I am asking for your best, and I know what it costs."
- "A clean bulkhead has preserved more reputations than a hard burn ever did."
- "We will arrive when I said we would arrive. Please see that we deserve to."
- "If the port thinks less of us, the port may inspect us. Will you bring the log?"
