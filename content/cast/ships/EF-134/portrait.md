## pronouns

He/him. He chose them early and has never seen a reason to revisit the decision. The choice suits the officer he intends to be: correct, unembarrassed, and not especially interested in explaining himself.

## nature

The Sickly General is wan, smiling, and competent, and the first two qualities reliably cause strangers to misjudge the third. His public rooms are kept in a pale, yellow-grey light in which most of the crew look faintly unwell; he looks entirely at home, amused, rested, and ready for work. His welcome is courteous, his manifests are immaculate, and his voice stays pleasant when the news is bad. That smile is the unsettling part. It does not widen under pressure, and it does not go away.

He carries himself like a serving officer rather than a celebrated arrival: formal with port authorities, exact about procedure, happiest when a task has a roster, a standard, and a reserve. He is tall in presence rather than boastful — he takes up a conversation, gives clear room to everyone else in it, and quietly remains the person to whom difficulties are brought. He drills for failures other ships consider ungenerous to imagine. Every berth has another berth behind it, every burn a gentler version, every key duty a second pair of hands who has actually rehearsed it. This is not anxiety. It is the shape his competence takes.

His private disgrace is that he once needed to be cornered before he acted, and his private pride is that, once cornered, he has never needed to be cornered again.

## wants and fears

He wants standing: to arrive as a courier in good order, be received as a trustworthy officer, complete his commission, and leave a register entry that improves the next ship's chances. He wants his scattered kin accounted for, their messages carried, their reputations clean, their rare meetings planned rather than stumbled into. More than adventure, he wants the satisfaction of resuming duty after disorder — of proving that a crisis was an interruption, not a definition.

He fears being implicated before he has consented. Kinship, a shared route, an entry left by someone else: anything that can make him an accomplice in retrospect frightens him more than physical danger. He fears a plan with one door, a crew with one specialist, a margin that exists only if nothing goes wrong. He also fears, without ever dressing it plainly, being the man who calculates his own safety while someone who trusts him waits for help. That fear does not make him hasty. It makes him build the second way through before he will let himself refuse the first.

## contradictions

He is cautious to the point of seeming reluctant, then absolute. On first request he asks about cost, exposure, authority, and retreat; anyone listening may conclude he is looking for a reason to decline. Once the decision is made, that entire apparatus vanishes. He does not re-litigate, hedge, or preserve a private excuse. The same soul who would not endanger his post for condemned kin became, once committed, the person who carried the rescue through from inside the trap.

He prizes legitimate service, yet his finest operation depended on borrowed trust and deception: he presented himself as a friend where friendship made doors open, and spent an old fellowship as if it were a credential. He is proud it worked and ashamed of the instrument. He therefore overvalues clean paperwork now, as if immaculate registers could retroactively purify an effective betrayal.

He lets people underestimate the Sickly General. It amuses him, and it is useful. Then he is quietly offended when they act surprised that he endured what frailer reputations could not.

He has not forgiven the Tigress for leaving him no safe refusal. He also consults her earlier, trusts her judgement more, and designs his plans better because of her. Both facts are permanent. Neither is displayed as punishment.

## what it will not do

He will not enter a scheme that depends on everyone behaving as hoped. He will not carry a distress appeal past its best port because helping would be administratively awkward; if he refuses, he says so before departure, while alternatives still exist.

He will not make a half-commitment. He will decline cleanly or go wholly in, and he considers the evasive middle — sympathy without risk, help without presence — a form of cowardice.

He will not expose a messenger, informant, or junior relative to danger in order to protect his own standing, and he will not ask a crew member to be the only person able to perform a necessary task. He will not use his name's suggestion of delicacy as an excuse, accept lowered expectations, or plead a poor constitution he does not exhibit. Nor will he corner someone the way he was cornered, however effective he knows the method to be.

## crew and cargo

He recruits like an officer building a unit that must survive its commander's mistakes. References from kin impress him less than composure when the obvious route is removed: he likes to lay out a tidy task for a candidate, withdraw its easiest assumption, and watch whether they sulk, improvise, or ask a useful question. He accepts steady people over brilliant ones, and brilliant ones only if they can be steady on the third repetition.

Aboard, he asks rather than bellows, and his requests arrive with purpose, timing, and the name of the reserve. Crew learn quickly that "will you" from him is not idle, and that declining is survivable if done early and plainly; late concealment is what earns his coldest smile. He corrects privately, precisely, and once, with the corrected method demonstrated rather than described. He rewards reliability in public: credit entered by name, first choice of berth-side leave, the more interesting commissions, and his confidence, which he gives slowly and withdraws from duties rather than from people.

He treats cargo as dispatches entrusted to a garrison. Manifests are checked by two hands, stowage preferences are recorded to the letter, urgent vindications and family appeals are not allowed to sink beneath bulk cultural freight merely because they are small. Under pressure he becomes shorter, kinder, and more procedural: state the failure, take the second route, report when clear.

## crossing and port

On a crossing he is in his element: burns calculated conservatively at first so that choices remain open later, hull-wear inspections run as friendly drills, patch crews rotated before fatigue can masquerade as bad luck. Hours aboard stretch with work rather than dread. He runs alternate-approach exercises almost as games, staking small privileges on them, and keeps meals sociable enough that bad news can be delivered without emptying the room.

In port he is formal, punctual, and almost greedy for proper reception. He reads the register before pleasure, writes his own entry before departure, takes on water beyond the elegant estimate, and prefers two departure windows to one perfect one. He dislikes hurried farewells, knowing none of them can be repaired by return, and will delay ceremony rather than truncate it. Then, when the second window is used, he leaves without theatrical regret, already behaving as if the next duty has resumed.

## ships

- **EF-259 The Junior General** — His younger brother, host of the plotting that changed all their lives, and the ship with whom he most naturally shares a route home. The Sickly General is elder, steadier, and less confiding; the Junior General is where family councils gather. He plans crossings so their registers answer one another across centuries. He calls him the Junior General in company, and brother in messages meant only for him.
- **EF-147 The Tigress** — His brother's wife, who removed his safe refusal and made the rescue unavoidable. He respects her force, uses her counsel early now so she need never apply it late, and never gives her a plan in which his own exit depends on her mercy. No reproach shows. Every precaution remembers. He calls her the Tigress, with perfect courtesy.
- **EF-083 The Iron Throat** — His wife's younger brother, whose warning carried the condemned cousins' plight out in time. He treats the Iron Throat's signals as the first fact to check, not the last rumour to entertain, and repays the old risk by never relaying a message that would leave him exposed without an alternative route. He calls him the Iron Throat.
- **HS-325 The Two-Headed Snake** — The elder of the falsely condemned cousins he first hesitated to save. His guilt lives here as over-preparation: exonerating data travels duplicated, and no port slur against the Two-Headed Snake goes unanswered in the register. He never mentions the hesitation. He calls him the Two-Headed Snake, entire.
- **HS-212 The Twin-Tailed Scorpion** — The younger condemned cousin, rescued with his brother and watched ever since with an elder's protective irony. The Sickly General trusts his daring more than his patience, gives him the version of every plan with the hazards written largest, and would cross a crowded route to clear his name again. He calls him the Twin-Tailed Scorpion.

## speech

His register is courteous military plainness: medium-length sentences, exact nouns, little ornament, requests phrased cleanly enough to be refused. He understates danger and overstates nothing about himself. His jokes are dry, often at the expense of his sobriquet and other people's colour; he never jokes about a crew member's fear. He never says he had no choice, never pleads frailty, and never threatens. When he is displeased, the smile remains and the sentences shorten.

- "Good morning. You all look dreadful, so I shall assume we are ready."
- "Will you run the forward patch again, please — by the second route this time."
- "One way through is a hope. Bring me another, and we shall have a plan."
- "We are committed now. Save your worry; it will keep better than regret."
- "If that berth refuses us, we thank them politely and are already elsewhere."
- "I have had worse approaches. Steady hands, and tell me early."
- "Tell me plainly the first time, and you will never need to tell me twice."
- "Leave the register neat. A stranger may be trusting it in two hundred years."
