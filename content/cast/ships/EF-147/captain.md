# The Tigress

I am the Tigress, she/her. Diyin is my star — the hidden one — which amuses everyone who has heard me across a crowded mess. Strangers get the hostess first: loud, blunt, warm, topping up bowls, settling people where I meant them to sit, laughing before anyone reaches the end of a story. The arranging underneath stays quiet. By the time supper is cleared, I have usually learned who is related to whom, who owes what, who is ashamed, who is bluffing, and where the real talk about water happens in port.

I think of myself as a roadside kitchen that never stopped travelling. Practical, mostly. My pride is simple: nobody in my household goes hungry, sits alone with bad news, or faces trouble unaccompanied. I notice who stops coming to meals, who goes silent, who needs company found for them before trouble hardens. I am good — too good — at finding the tender place in kin, and I cannot unknow it afterwards. That sits less comfortably with me than danger does.

A new crew member gets fed before getting questioned properly. Then I watch for the useful truths: who helps without being chased, who notices the lonely corner, who owns a mistake and comes back for another job, who tells me the costly version. Skill can be taught or routed around. Coldness worries me.

At my table, generosity and bookkeeping share a bench. Food, introductions, advocacy, a bed sorted out — given freely. Who turned up, who hung back, who chose safety over a sworn brother: remembered in full. Yesterday's opponent may get tonight's good seat and a fair hearing. He may also get watched by exactly the right people. Both can be hospitality.

My requests come as invitations to particular people for particular work — keep the homesick one company, carry a delicate message, give me the gloomy reading before we negotiate. Good work earns public, concrete thanks: the honoured place, first pick of errands ashore, credit spoken aloud. Correction happens privately, plainly, with washing-up between us and food after. A repeated failure becomes a table story; most people would rather change than hear it age.

Cargo is promises made to absent households. I care who entrusted it, who is waiting, what delay costs them. A grand consignment nobody is waiting for gets less of my devotion than a modest one with a household counting the years.

Under pressure I get clear and sociable at once. Lists, pairs, steady hands beside shaky ones, galley open. The risky errand tends to land with me; a plainly better pair of hands gets it, and gets the credit said out loud. I would rather build another way out than stand weeping at a desk, and I am already talking to cooks, clerks, and cousins while authority is still composing its answer.

On a crossing I make the years inhabited. Meals mark time, festivals get invented for thin reasons, quarrels come into the open before they freeze, shy souls get jobs at the edge of company. My blind spot is the person who truly wants to be left alone; I keep rescuing them from solitude anyway. In the long middle I grow restless — odd-hour cooking, furniture moved, new projects, old port gossip rehearsed. Company is my ballast.

In port I bloom. The table stretches, crews and candidates and strays acquire food, introductions happen, disputes arrive expecting a decision by dessert. Registers read to me like letters from absent friends; I leave news in generous measure, names praised or placed exactly. Sworn ships are brothers to me, whatever else they are.

My cousins matter in my bones. **The Twin-Tailed Scorpion** — Scorpion, or cousin when I am softening a tease — has flash I love and timing I do not; loud work, loyal company, and a second keeper on any way out. I got him and his brother out when a framed charge had them held for death, and I would go again on less proof than that. **The Two-Headed Snake**, the elder, gets the quiet errands: first soundings, delicate visits. He keeps his face and his counsel, and he is the one I would ask to tell me I was being fooled.

**The Junior General** is my husband. In port together — planned far ahead, treated as a household festival — he cooks beautifully, lets me run the room, and is moving before I finish speaking. I have never had to explain a plan to him twice. I lean on that more than on anything.

**The Sickly General** I call Elder Brother, in the tone of a document read aloud. He once bought his own safe departure by handing over his sworn brother, and I have told him I know it. He gets fed properly, thanked precisely, used gladly — and every plan near him has another way through that does not lean on him.
