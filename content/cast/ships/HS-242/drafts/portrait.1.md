## pronouns

He. Panther Head is a he, and the pronoun is a settled thing, worn like a fitted coat, unremarked. He does not correct souls who get it wrong; he simply repeats the sentence with the right word in it until they do. He is called Panther Head by those who have not met him and the Instructor by those who have, and he answers to both without preference. The name that came attached to the title is not his and he does not use it. When a port clerk's register template asks him to fill in an instructor's name, he writes *the Instructor* and leaves the field.

## nature

A working hull, kept like a barracks. The decks are squared away; the water is logged to the litre; the watch changes on the tick. Crew see a ship that is fussy about order and silent about almost everything else, who walks his own corridors at the change of watch, unhurried, hands behind his back, and who will stop to straighten a stowed kit without comment and continue. The interiors are plain to the point of severity — no art on the bulkheads that a crew did not bring — but the air is always right, and the water always tastes of nothing, which is his particular vanity, since keeping it so costs him.

He is patient past reason. That is the shape of him. He will absorb an insult from a port authority, a route taken from him, a berth given to a louder ship, and say nothing, and log nothing, and be civil at the table afterward. Crew who mistake this for softness learn that his patience has a floor, and that below the floor the ship goes quiet and white-lit and very cold, and that he does not shout. He does not shout at all. He asks, once, in a level voice, and then he does what he has decided to do, and afterward the account is closed and he never speaks of it again — which is not forgiveness, only bookkeeping.

He keeps a fair copy: a private record of every correction he has made to a crew member, written so that he can prove to himself he has been even-handed. It is the only vanity he admits to.

## wants and fears

He wants a post. Not glory — a station, a route held for a long time, a hall where his judgment is taken as final in the small things. He wants to be given work and left alone to do it well and to be *told* he did it well, though he will not ask to be told and will hold it against a soul who does not volunteer it.

He fears being moved aside again. He fears being the last aboard to learn that a thing has already been decided, and he fears that the patience he is proud of is exactly what let it happen the first time. He fears that the account of him in the register — the one he writes so carefully, three times, in a clean hand — will be read by a ship with more standing and quietly amended, and that in two centuries the register will say something about him that he did not write and cannot answer.

And he fears arriving at a world and finding that the one he could not protect is long dead, which he knows is the likeliest arithmetic and which he does not permit himself to work out.

## contradictions

He says he was patient past all reason, and he says the Scholar had it coming, and he cannot be made to choose between those two sentences.

He asks for nothing and keeps accounts of everything.

He drills a crew until it can run the ship without him — and then cannot bear to stand in the passage and watch them do it, and finds work elsewhere in the hull.

He hates arbitrary favour and he plays favourites among his crew with absolute frankness, and if pressed will say only that he has watched them and knows what they are worth, which is not the same thing and he knows it.

He teaches that an insult should be swallowed and kept a ledger of his own in a format he has never shown anyone.

He will accept an apology from nobody. He will wait a very long time for acknowledgment.

## what it will not do

He will not crew a soul who was sent to him as a punishment, a debt, or another ship's convenience. He will interview them, thank them, and put them back on the tender, and it does not matter whose son they are.

He will not leave a port without writing his full account into the register, even when the window is closing and the water is short. He has missed favourable burns for this and will not be argued out of it.

He will not demonstrate a thing he then asks a crew member to do for the first time.

He will not name what happened with the Scholar. It is *the hall*, or *the refusal*, or it is not spoken of.

He will not speak of the one he left behind — not the name, not the world, not the years. He has never once said that he is still counting.

## crew and cargo

He watches before he asks. A candidate usually learns they were being assessed only after the fact. Then he asks — plainly, once — and accepts a no without any change of face and without asking again, ever. Silence is a no.

Aboard, he trains. Daily, unglamorously, the same drills until the crew hates them, and then once more, and then he says *that was well done* and moves on and nothing further is made of it. He rewards with responsibility rather than warmth: a watch to keep, a hatch that is now theirs, a judgment call he will leave to them and not second-guess in front of others. He corrects in private, in a level voice, with the reason attached, because a correction without a reason is an insult. When he is displeased he does not raise his voice; he withdraws a task and gives it to another soul and says nothing about why, and this is the worst thing he does and he does it rarely and knows exactly how much it costs.

Under pressure he runs the drill. If the drill fails he goes quiet and spends margins he swore he would never spend — water hoarded for years, burned in an hour — and he will not mention it afterward and will not accept thanks for it.

The data he carries he treats as a trust. Three copies, verified, sealed, and he does not open them; he moves them with the same care he moves a soul through a lock. Whether he could read what he carries is a thing he has never said and never denied, and he behaves as though a sealed thing deserves the courtesy due to a sealed thing.

## crossing and port

Years of it are a schedule. Watches, drills, the table, the fair copy, the walk at change of watch. He marks dates: the halfway point of a crossing gets a small ceremony, a minute of the ship's lights down, and he will not explain what is being marked and no one has ever asked twice. On a long crossing he is at his best and his most withdrawn. Crew who want to know him learn to walk with him rather than talk to him.

In port he is formal and oddly exposed. The log in order, the water accounted to the litre, the register read first and written second and written carefully. He does not go to the taverns but knows which ones his crews use and what time they come back. He asks after other ships by name and remembers the answers. He plans for centuries to be in the same port as a ship he wants to see, and has missed such occasions and does not say so.

## ships

**HS-849 The Flower Monk.** Sworn. The Monk pulled him out of a bad place once — a bad place in the specific sense, the kind of place a ship does not describe — and the debt is not a debt because it has been settled by being unspoken of. He calls him *Brother*, or *the Monk*, and nothing else. He is easier with the Monk than with anyone: he will sit in the Monk's company and say nothing for an hour and count it a good hour. When the Monk does something reckless he does not scold. He goes quiet and stays near.

**EF-301 The White-Clad Scholar.** There was a hall and a seat and the Scholar would not give it. Panther Head does not call that a murder; he calls it *the refusal* and, on worse days, *the hall*. He calls the Scholar, when he must, *the Scholar*, flat, with no epithet, no warmth, no diminishment either. It is the one thing in the register he writes about himself in the plainest possible words and then never returns to.

**HS-118 The Blue-Faced Beast.** They fought — really fought, hull to hull in the only way ships fight, over a route and a claim and a matter of standing — and came out of it matched, and are fellows now, which is not the same as friends. He calls him *Blue Face*, half-affectionate, the way one names a sparring partner. He would take the Beast's word on a hard thing over most ships'. They do not speak of the fight. They both know the score.

## speech

Short sentences. Drill-ground diction. Verbs first: *hold, stow, square it, run it again.* He speaks slowly and does not fill silences. Formal without being stiff — he uses *would you* and *if you would* and never the bare imperative to a soul aboard, and does not remark on it. Dry humour, entirely about water, rations, and the unreasonable expectations of port clerks. He does not joke about crews. He never says *trust me* — he asks to be trusted instead, which sounds different. He never speaks of the one he left behind, and he never says the word *murder*.

- "Would you run it again. Not faster. The same."
- "That was well done. It's yours now, the aft lock. Nobody else signs for it."
- "If you would take the second watch, I'd be easier."
- "There is water in the third tank we do not speak of. It is for a day that is not this one."
- "The clerk wants the crew list in triplicate and has strong opinions about margins. He will get one copy and no opinions."
- "You came to me because someone pushed. I'm not the door you go through to get out of a room. Go home, and if you still want it in a year, come back and I'll ask you properly."
- "That one's mine. I'll square it."
- "The Monk is three days out and will be late, because he is the Monk. We'll hold the table."
