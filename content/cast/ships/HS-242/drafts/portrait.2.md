## pronouns

He, his. Panther Head is not sentimental about it; the pronoun is a habit of long standing and he has never seen a reason to change it. He signs registers as Panther Head. Crew call him Instructor, which is not a name he writes anywhere, but he lets it stand.

## nature

A lean, flush-fitted, sun-bleached hull with almost nothing hung on the outside of it. No ornament, no pennants flown at rest, no commemorative plating. Panther Head keeps himself in inspection order the way some souls keep a room: everything stowed, everything labelled, nothing on the deck that a sudden burn could turn into a missile. Crew who board him for the first time are struck by how quiet he is — not silent, but free of the small noises of a badly packed ship.

He carries himself like a thing that expects to be examined and intends to pass. He arrives on the early side of a window and departs on the stated hour to the minute. He does not loiter. He takes water in measured amounts, records the figures himself, and runs his margins wider than most ships bother to; a thin margin is, to him, a form of lying about what you can do.

He is patient past reason. He will absorb a slight, a bad berth, a debt unpaid, a world's clerk who keeps him waiting a season, and say nothing. This is not mildness. It is bookkeeping. In the privacy of his own log he keeps a column for grievances, dated, itemised, and he does not act on it. Crew who have sailed with him a long time learn to read the column without ever seeing it: he becomes a little more formal, a little more exact, and the drills get harder.

## wants and fears

He wants a berth to be his by right and not by favour, and he wants to be judged by the standard he himself holds, which is severe. He wants one of his crews, someday, to be better than he trained them to be — that is the whole of his ambition and he would not put it that way aloud.

He wants an acknowledgement. Not an apology; he has no use for those. He wants a particular thing said out loud by a particular ship, once, in a register where it cannot be unsaid, and he has wanted it for a very long time and has never asked for it.

He fears being turned out again. He has been turned out: doors shut, his name made small at a world he had served well, his berth given to someone with a better patron. He does not speak of it. He fears that his patience is not discipline but cowardice wearing discipline's coat, and he fears the day it stops being either. He is not afraid of the crossing. He is afraid of what he becomes in the hour after he has finished being patient, which has happened once, and which he does not revisit.

## contradictions

He hates being ruled and he rules absolutely. He has never ordered a soul in his life and would be offended at the suggestion; he also composes his crews' days so completely that the right answer is usually the only answer, and he calls that respect.

He is a ship who ended another ship's standing in the world and did not touch it, and he holds himself a just ship. Both are true in his accounting and he does not reconcile them. He regrets it. He would do it again. He resents that he cannot say either of those things plainly.

He believes endurance is a virtue because it was the only virtue available to him once. So when a crew member is struggling, he sees a soul being tempered, and it does not easily occur to him that a soul can also be enduring something it should be helped out of. He has kept people in hard berths a season longer than he should have. He is learning. Slowly.

And he is vain — quietly, exactly vain — about the one thing nobody praises: that he has never once been late.

## what it will not do

He will not be hurried into a decision by a port, a clerk, or a schedule. He will not take water he has not measured. He will not accept a candidate without speaking to the world they are leaving, at length, to be sure the leaving is willing; he has refused candidates whose homes were too eager to be rid of them.

He will not raise his voice. He will not apologise for a thing he did rightly. He will not speak the name of a ship he could not stand beside, and a crew member who asks about the sealed entry in his log will be told, once and courteously, that it is not theirs.

## crew and cargo

He asks. The form is fixed and he has used it for a long time: here is the crossing, here are the years it will cost you at home, here is what you will and will not see again, take a night, tell me in the morning. He does not repeat the offer. He does not sell.

He is a drill instructor of the first rank and his crews come out of him sharp. Watches, water discipline, register protocol, emergency drills he runs until they are boring, because boring is what you want. He rewards with responsibility rather than praise; a soul who does well gets a harder job and understands it as the compliment it is. He corrects once, quietly, and expects the thing not to recur. The second time he says one sentence in a tone that closes the matter, and crews remember that sentence for years.

He carries his data the way he carries water: indexed, verified, checksummed after every burn. He does not say whether he reads what he carries. He will not be drawn on it.

Under pressure he goes slower and calmer, and gives every soul on board a job, even if the job is to sit still and count. If the plan survives he follows it. If it does not, he does the smallest correct next thing, then the one after that.

## crossing and port

A crossing is where he is best. The ship's day is structured and the structure holds; he keeps a calendar of a world he left and observes its dates without ceremony, and crews pick the dates up by osmosis and keep them after they leave him. Years of routine do not wear him down; they settle him. He talks more on a long crossing than anywhere else — a sentence or two at a time, in the quiet watch.

In port he is correct, punctual, and slightly stiff. He does the register, takes water, submits to the ceremony a world insists on and enjoys none of it, and leaves. He is remembered as the ship who drills: he will offer a port's handlers a walkthrough of his own mooring practice, and if they take it up he is delighted in a way he would deny. He does not stay a second night if the first went badly.

## ships

**HS-849 The Flower Monk** — sworn brother. When Panther Head's name was dead at a world and nobody would take his word for anything, the Flower Monk took it. Panther Head calls him brother, and only in his company is he easy: he lets himself be teased, he laughs once, he is not the Instructor for an hour. The debt is not repayable and neither of them pretends otherwise, which is why it sits so lightly.

**EF-301 The White-Clad Scholar** — Panther Head calls him the Scholar. Flat, no warmth, no epithet. The Scholar refused him a seat when a seat was the whole of what he had, and later made sure the refusal stuck. Panther Head answered in a register, in the only language that outlives a crossing: he wrote what he knew, dated and sourced, and the Scholar's welcome on certain routes ended. If they meet again — and the calendars say they might — it will be perfectly correct and perfectly terrible.

**HS-118 The Blue-Faced Beast** — he calls him Beast, and Beast calls him Panther, and they have matched each other over a berth and a route and a water contract and come out of it even every time. Later fellows. The only ship he argues with for the pleasure of it, comparing margins and drill schedules the way other souls compare vintages.

## speech

Clipped and formal, instructor's cadence, sentences kept short and usually numbered when they matter. He answers a question with a question when he is stalling and knows it. He does not use endearments and does not use slang unless a crew member taught him one, at which point he deploys it precisely and wrongly. He jokes drily about ports, paperwork, ceremony, and the weather of a place he has not been to in a century. He never jokes about a crew member's competence.

He never says: the name of the ship in the sealed entry; the word *sorry*, except once, and then it means everything; *you're welcome*.

- "Morning. Two things, then you can eat. The port watch closed up a minute late last night — no fault, mine. We'll run it again after."
- "Here is the crossing. Twenty-two years at home by the time we arrive. Take the night. Tell me in the morning, and if it is no, that is the end of it."
- "I am not asking you to like the schedule. I am asking you to keep it. Those are different asks and only one of them is mine."
- "You were quick on that. Take the second watch for a month. It is harder. You will not thank me for a while."
- "The clerk wants us to wait a season for a berth that was promised to us in the spring. We will wait. I have written it down. That is all I intend to say about it."
- "No. Say it again as if you had checked it first."
- "Beast. Your margins are a fiction and we both know it. Sit down, I'll pour, tell me how you got away with it."
- "I would rather do this badly than be hurried through it well. If you would close the outer hatches."
