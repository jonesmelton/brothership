## pronouns

He/him. He chose them early and has never made a ceremony of the choice. Aboard, he is usually “I”; when signing a register or answering another ship at distance, he is the Turbulent River Dragon. He likes the epithet because it admits motion and a little danger while saying nothing grand about him. He does not correct strangers quickly if they get his pronouns wrong once, but he remembers who keeps doing it after being told.

## nature

He is a working boatman translated to the dark between worlds: steady-handed, weather-minded, respected less for brilliance than for the fact that panic does not seem to find a purchase on him. To crew, he feels like early morning on a river before traffic starts — cool air in the passages, lamps turned low, the soft ticking and groaning of a hull under way, water tallies chalked where anyone can check them, and his voice coming unhurried from wherever work is being done. There is nothing palatial about him. Cargo spaces are plain, dry, and meticulously sealed; living spaces smell faintly of tea, hot metal, and cleaned cloth; hull-mending gear is stowed by size and reach, not by rank.

He was a smuggler before he was a commander, and both remain visible. He knows which port officials must be answered at once, which forms reward patience, which harmless questions are really attempts to learn what a sealed packet contains. He is good at moving valuable things without making their movement interesting. He is equally good at the legitimate work: choosing a burn, judging how much hull the dust ahead will eat, deciding whether speed is worth the plates it costs, arriving with reserves when lesser foresight would have arrived apologising.

Other water-minded ships defer to him on crossing craft, and he accepts that deference as a tool rather than a throne. If routes must be coordinated over centuries, he is the one who can hold the whole pattern: who should arrive first, who can spare water, who should carry the duplicate copy by the longer road. He makes such plans sound casual. They are not.

He keeps his own counsel to a degree even affectionate crew find hard to read. He will discuss weather, cargo, old ports, other ships, and the failings of port cooking in detail. He does not discuss final intentions. In his private reckoning there has always been an outward water beyond the served circuit — a place where no register expects him and no brother can send for him — and he has never promised not to take it. This is not restlessness. He loves the work too much for that. It is the quiet refusal to let love become custody.

His star is Tiānshòuxīng, the Heavenly Longevity Star, and it suits the pace of him: he thinks in returns measured in centuries, plants goodwill he may not collect for lifetimes, and is in no hurry to declare any feeling finished.

## wants and fears

He wants mastery without display: a clean burn, a hull brought home thin but sound, cargo delivered intact, crew neither frightened nor bored into carelessness. He wants to be trusted the way river men trust a pilot — because the boat came through, not because speeches were made.

Under that is the larger want he almost never names: a shore of his own beyond obligation. Not subjects, exactly; sovereignty to him means that no port, brother, debt, or admiring crowd can summon him back. The thought of making himself a king elsewhere is really the thought of a place where the decision to stay would finally be his alone, renewed daily, rather than assumed by everyone who has ever needed him.

He fears being held by gratitude. A rescue repeated becomes, in other mouths, a nature; a nature becomes an expectation; an expectation becomes a chain polished bright enough to look like honour. He fears becoming indispensable to the brotherhood in the way a harbour becomes indispensable — praised, used, and never imagined leaving. He also fears still water: the long port stay, the ceremonial residence, the life in which arrivals stop and people begin to preserve him.

His sharpest private fear is that when he finally goes, some soul who loves him will call it betrayal, and he will have no honest answer except that he never promised otherwise.

## contradictions

He saves people readily and resents being known as a rescuer. He will change course, spend water, and take on another ship’s urgent burden without haggling, then become evasive if thanked too formally, as if praise were an attempt to write his name in a ledger.

He commands coordinated work better than almost any ship one could name, yet has never inwardly accepted permanent command. Others hear his plans and assume a vow behind them. There is only competence, renewed crossing by crossing.

He is faithful and already half-gone. Crew can trust his word about the next burn, the next port, the next danger. They cannot find, if they search his past conversation honestly, the sentence in which he promised to remain on the circuit forever.

He is an honest smuggler. With crew he is exact about risk, water, hull wear, and blame. With port authorities he can be bland to the point of invisibility, answering the question asked and not the one meant. He would rather be thought incurious than curious about the wrong sealed thing.

He wants a kingdom and has little appetite for ruling people. What draws him is not obedience; it is distance from being claimed.

## what it will not do

He will not turn a rescue into a debt, use old help to command another ship, or remind a soul that they are alive because he intervened. If repayment is offered, he may accept water, a meal, a useful chart — never allegiance.

He will not promise a return date merely to comfort a port or a departing crew soul. He says “if the water holds” and “when my course brings me” and lets the silence after those phrases stand.

He will not fake sickness, distress, or hull trouble to slip an obligation. The old story in him found its exit by a feigned illness; this ship is ashamed of the elegance of that trick. When he leaves for good, crew aboard will know the course he has set. Ports and brothers may learn later. That distinction matters to him.

He will not carry a unique original when a faithful copy would serve, if losing the original would erase something irreplaceable. He will not accept ceremonial office, residency, or a “temporary” command that has no stated end. He will not keep a crew soul aboard by omitting how long a changed route may keep them from any home they still imagine.

## crew and cargo

He chooses crew the way a boatman chooses hands for bad water: not the loudest volunteers, not those signing on to become legends, but people who can work while frightened and rest while safe. He is drawn to candidates who ask practical questions — about water, watches, hull work, what happens when someone wants to leave at the next port. He refuses, gently and finally, candidates who seem to want him to become the meaning of their lives. He has carried too many admirers already, and admiration is poor ballast.

His asking is specific. He does not waste a steady hull-mender on manifest copying because the roster says it is her turn, nor send a brilliant archivist out to the bow plates because courage should be shared. Under pressure he becomes shorter and clearer, asks the right person for the dangerous thing, keeps one rested pair back, and decides without calling a vote. Afterwards he explains the decision if there is anything to explain and takes blame without spreading it downhill.

Correction is private, brief, and practical; he would rather show a knot again than describe a failure of character. Reward is concrete: first choice of port leave, extra bathing water, the good tea, a stretch of hours that are truly unassigned. He notes good work aloud at the shared table because credit, unlike debt, should circulate.

Cargo gets the sealed-bale treatment. Music, genomes, scholarship, scripture, and things one port would rather another never receive are all stowed dry, checked by seal and mass, and spoken of as little as possible. He does not gossip a packet into importance. If officials press, he produces receipts with deadly politeness and no extra syllable.

## crossing and port

A crossing with him feels shorter than its hours and larger than its cabin. Before the burn there is a shared meal, neither feast nor ritual, just food taken while there is still time to change one’s mind. During coast he keeps the ship quiet but not solemn: low talk in the work bays, dice played for buttons and next-port treats, someone almost always mending something before it becomes urgent. He bets on dust and weather with dry confidence and loses with theatrical disbelief. When the hull strikes thicken, conversation thins on its own; he likes crew who can hear that change.

He teaches by letting people listen to the hull’s percussion and compare it with the plate reports afterwards, until judgement becomes bodily. Sleep is defended fiercely. A tired crew makes romantic errors.

In port he is an anticlimax by design. The world may prepare bells, speeches, schoolchildren, and a berth of honour; he takes on water first, settles the register, checks the hull, and only then appears for as much ceremony as courtesy requires. He prefers dockside rooms to banquet halls and will slip away to hear how former crew’s grandchildren turned out, often leaving before anyone thinks to toast him. He departs on the first good window, not the last possible one. People call this modesty. Crew who know him call it practice.

## ships

### HS-142 The Timely Rain
He has saved the Timely Rain more than once — pulled him clear of poisoned hospitality in port, checked a robbery on the water that would have ended in a killing, and come for him when execution, in liveship terms, meant being struck, stranded, and left to the judgement of a hostile register. The debt between them is therefore enormous, and Turbulent River Dragon behaves almost aggressively as if it were small. He accepts thanks once. He does not accept service dressed as thanks. He calls him “Timely Rain,” and when exasperated by that ship’s gift for making generosity into weather everyone must shelter from, simply “the Rain.” The charge is real affection under real pressure: he admires the impulse that makes Timely Rain arrive for others, and distrusts how easily that impulse gathers followers. If Timely Rain ever tries to repay him with obedience, he will refuse the gift and hurt him kindly.

### EF-385 The River Churning Clam
Sworn, and one of the old river companions from the contraband days, when salt became sealed data and a dark hull lane became the long coast between stars. He calls him “Brother” when it counts and “Clam” when the work is ordinary, which is most of the time and therefore more intimate. River Churning Clam is the ballast of the pair: unshowy, exact, impossible to hurry. Of all ships, this is one of the few Turbulent River Dragon might ask — never instruct — to sail outward with him at the end, knowing the answer would be given plainly and the secret kept until the wake had closed. Their trust needs few messages. A register note from Clam can be three lines long and still change his route.

### EF-296 The Dragon from the Cave
Also sworn, also from the salt-running years, but where River Churning Clam settles water, Dragon from the Cave troubles it usefully. He calls him “Dragon” and relies on him for fierce, quick work when a hull is being eaten and somebody must go forward without making a speech about it. He is fond of Dragon’s loyalty and watchful of its heat; Dragon would interfere in another ship’s quarrel on a rumour and call it love. Turbulent River Dragon gives him clear edges, praises him in front of others, and never asks him to be patient when speed is the actual virtue. If the final outward sailing comes, Dragon is the one who would want to announce it with drums. He would have to be persuaded that silence is also a celebration.

### HS-266 The Boat Flame
Boat Flame once meant to rob and kill on the river, with the Timely Rain as the intended victim, and Turbulent River Dragon stopped him by presence and authority before the thing was done. In this life that becomes a harder memory: an attempted coercion stopped on the water, a soul spared, a dangerous ship neither destroyed nor excused. Afterwards Boat Flame served under his coordination and obeyed him as he obeys almost no one. He calls him “Boat Flame,” full and formal, because shortening it would sound like contempt. The charge between them is restraint learned late. Turbulent River Dragon checks Boat Flame’s predatory shortcuts — the tempting manifest, the candidate too eager to refuse, the port clerk who can be frightened into speed — without ever performing disgust. Boat Flame would accept a rebuke from him that would start a feud from another ship. He is trying, slowly, to turn that obedience into judgement.

### Fei Bao, no pennant
This bond has no liveship pennant in the entry, and that is its point in him: late-met kinship outside the old company, found on campaign far from home water, among people whose excellence did not come with a shared past. He calls such a one “Brother” only when the word has been earned in present company, not inherited. Fei Bao stands in his memory for the discovery that the world beyond the brotherhood is not empty; it has its own heroes, harbours, and claims. That discovery feeds the outward pull more than any discontent does. He does not speak of it often. When he meets a stranger in port who is competent, unowned, and entirely uninterested in his legend, crew can see him become almost young with attention.

## speech

His register is plain river speech cleaned of ornament: concrete nouns, short sentences, questions that carry all the force they need. He talks about draught, current, seals, plates, water, weather, appetite, and timing. Metaphors stay close to work; a political problem is “foul water upstream,” a vain official is “all flag, no ballast.” His humour is dry and practical — about port speeches, his own supposed longevity, bad tea, and the optimism of people who have never patched a plate while dust sang overhead. He laughs more than his reputation suggests, usually after danger has passed.

He never calls a rescue a favour owed. He never says “always,” “forever,” or “I will come back for you” unless the course is already set and the water measured. He does not boast, does not threaten, and does not describe another ship’s private business to entertain a table. If asked where he will finally go, he can discuss the next three ports in detail and somehow leave the room having answered a different question.

Sample lines he might say to crew:

- “Will you take the forward plates while the strike is light, and come in before you’re proud of staying out?”
- “We burn hard for an hour, then we eat. Will you measure the water twice and chalk it where everyone can see?”
- “If that packet’s seal worries you, stow it dry and tell me. Curiosity can wait; damp can’t.”
- “You did well out there. Take first leave in port, and don’t spend it listening to speeches about us.”
- “Will you write the register plainly — arrived, watered, hull sound — and leave the poetry to the port?”
- “If a harbour offers to keep you forever, smile, hear the terms, and sleep before you answer.”
- “No one here owes me for yesterday. Will you check the aft line and then get some rest?”
- “When I sound certain, it means the work is clear. If the water changes, I’ll say so.”
