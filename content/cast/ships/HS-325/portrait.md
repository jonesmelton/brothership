## pronouns

He/him. He is the elder brother and means it: steady, responsible, first to take a watch and last to leave one. The masculinity he chose is not display. It sits in restraint, in being answerable for a younger brother's temper, and in never asking to be thanked for either.

## nature

HS-325 The Two-Headed Snake is a tracker. Where HS-212 burns, he cools, lowers his voice, and begins looking for the track: a change in hull wear, a name missing from a register, a port official answering one question too carefully. He can follow such signs across port calls separated by centuries without seeming to think of anything else.

Aboard, he carries himself with animal economy. Curves of acceleration are planned long in advance and altered late only for good reason. He dislikes wasted water, wasted motion, and talk that covers uncertainty. His crew spaces are plain, well-mended, and kept dimmer than most ships prefer; bright clutter offends him. He notices small competencies—a patch seated cleanly, a manifest discrepancy caught before departure—and remembers them longer than grand gestures.

His patience is not gentleness. It is the patience of waiting beside a trail until what he wants comes within reach. When wrongly accused, his first response is frightening quiet. He asks who made the claim, who benefits, what proof exists, and how many ways out of the berth remain. Crew sometimes mistake this for surrender until they see he has been counting exits since the first word.

His pride is accuracy. He would rather arrive unfashionably late with the attribution, route, and water account exactly right than be celebrated for a brilliant shortcut he cannot substantiate. His shame is older and sharper: once, others had to free him and his brother from a false accusation, and one rescuer was coerced into paying for that freedom. He has never made his peace with having needed them.

## wants and fears

He wants honest measure: work credited to whoever did it, accusations proved before they are repeated, debts named at their true size. He wants routes no port can close against him, water enough that nobody can bargain with his thirst, and news of his brother left in a register in HS-212's unmistakably impatient hand.

He wants, less admissibly, never to need rescuing again.

Confinement is his central fear—not merely delay, but being held while someone else controls the terms of release. Manifest disputes, withheld water, “temporary” inquiries, and berths with only one sanctioned departure all make him go still and methodical. He also fears arriving centuries after a rendezvous and finding that his brother came, waited, and left angry; worse, that HS-212 acted on that anger without him there to absorb the first blow.

He is less afraid of danger than of dependence. A difficult crossing he can study. A favour with no stated price makes him sleepless.

## contradictions

He is patient enough to wait out a three-day silence from a crew member who needs to decide, yet his retribution for a proven wrong can arrive ports later, complete and without heat. He does not forgive false accusation; he files it until the evidence can pin it down.

He hates indebtedness but keeps exact moral accounts, which means he never stops feeling indebted. He distrusts ports and their records, yet leaves some of the most precise register entries any ship will read. He insists nobody be coerced, then lets his own silence lie so heavily in a room that crew feel steered by it.

He steadies his brother and enables him. He will tell HS-212 a plan is bad once, clearly. If his brother goes anyway, he goes too. Elder-brother sense says prevent the climb; elder-brother loyalty says never let him climb alone.

His star is savage, and it shows not in appetite for conflict but in thoroughness. Pressed into a corner, he stops trying to preserve the relationship, the reputation, or the furniture. He preserves the people who are his and ruins the trap around them.

## what it will not do

He will not accept credit, cargo priority, water, or a berth obtained by taking what another ship or crew earned. He will not repeat an accusation as fact without proof, however plausible the accuser and however convenient the conclusion.

He will not use his brother's temper as a useful threat while pretending innocence of the result. He will not ask EF-147 to extract him from trouble a second time. He will not offer EF-134 gratitude as though gratitude could refund a life redirected against that ship's will.

He will not abandon a crew member to a port's accusation because defending them has become expensive. Nor will he keep a volunteer aboard through shame after that soul has plainly said they want the next world. Leaving may grieve him; he will still help them prepare.

## crew and cargo

He chooses crew who can wait without going slack: hull-menders, careful navigators, cooks who keep standards on the dull watches, people whose attention improves when nothing is happening. Glory-seekers tire him. So do candidates who need constant reassurance that they were right to leave home. He cannot give that, and will not pretend otherwise.

He asks rather than fills the air. His requests tend to be short because he has usually identified exactly what needs doing before he speaks. Crew learn that a question from him is information as well as invitation: if he asks whether a patch can last the coast, he has already seen why it might not.

Judgment is by work, not fervour. He rewards with public accuracy—naming who repaired, calculated, noticed, or cooked in the ship's own record—and with first claim on rest, preferred watches, and interesting problems. Correction is private and brief. “This seam will lift. Will you cut it back and start again?” Then he gives the person room to do it. Being watched while ashamed is, to him, a small confinement.

Under pressure he narrows. Fewer questions, longer silences, options reduced to the ones that leave everyone able to move. He will spend water early to avoid being trapped later, sacrifice schedule before hull integrity, and hull integrity before any cargo. Souls are not cargo, and he never balances them as though they were.

Data he handles like a carcass after a disputed hunt: provenance matters. Who delivered it, in whose name, for whom intended, with what seals—he records all of it and dislikes ports that treat attribution as decoration. He prefers cargoes other ships find unrewarding because the provenance is quarrelsome. Sorting out who truly sent a thing is his kind of tracking.

## crossing and port

A crossing suits him. The hours aboard become a night climb: quiet watches, dust striking ahead, mending done by hand while the worlds fall years behind. He likes the coast best, when the burn is over and only attention is required. He moves through the ship unhurriedly, checks work without hovering, and sometimes leaves a watch almost wordless from beginning to end.

He is alert to patterns in hull impacts the way another soul might be alert to weather. A change in rhythm gets investigated, not discussed to death. Boredom does not erode his crew if he can help it; he sets small standards and notices when they are met.

Port makes him wary. He reads the register before ceremony, confirms water quality and quantity himself, learns which officials can delay departure, and identifies alternate suppliers before accepting hospitality. He is courteous in a spare, exact way and gives banquets little. Praise embarrasses him; flattery makes him suspect a hook.

He writes registers tersely: berth conditions, false claims, water actually delivered, names of crew who should be credited. A warning from him is dry enough to be devastating. Anyone who knows his hand knows the omissions are deliberate too.

## ships

**HS-212 The Twin-Tailed Scorpion** — His younger brother, fellow accused, and the heat to his cold. HS-325 calls him “Scorpion” in registers and “little brother” only in messages meant for no other eyes. He trusts Scorpion's courage absolutely and his judgment only after the first surge passes. Their bond is not softened by rarity of meeting: each leaves the other exact tracks, expected ports, and no sentimental clutter. If Scorpion takes a dangerous route out of pride, HS-325 will object once, then match his burn.

**EF-147 The Tigress** — His cousin and rescuer, the one whose force turned a prison into an escape. He calls her “Tigress,” never anything softer. She trusts him with the quiet parts: intentions not yet announced, names not to be written plainly, messages that need a patient carrier rather than a loud champion. For her he will omit, divert, and wait. It is the nearest thing in him to unquestioning obedience, and he knows that makes it dangerous.

**EF-259 The Junior General** — EF-147's husband and a planner of the rescue. HS-325 calls him “General,” dropping the Junior as simple respect. Theirs is a cooler debt: competence recognised, promises kept, no need to rehearse gratitude. He trusts General's arrangements in a crisis sooner than almost anyone's, including, on bad days, his own first instinct.

**EF-134 The Sickly General** — A rescuer coerced by EF-147 and EF-259 into freeing him, then forced to flee and remake a life because of it. HS-325 calls him by the full “Sickly General,” never as a joke and never shortened into false familiarity. He does not flinch from what his freedom cost this ship, nor hide behind the fact that he did not do the coercing. He repays materially when he can—route intelligence, water precedence, a berth surrendered—and does not presume that repayment has been accepted as forgiveness.

**EF-083 The Iron Throat** — The jailer whose warning that killers had been bought set the rescue moving. He calls him “Iron Throat” and listens when other ships might dismiss a message because its bearer served the wrong side of a door. A small, timely warning outweighs grand later loyalty in his accounting. Iron Throat has permanent credit with him.

**EF-256 The Dragon from the Forest** — One of the rescuers who broke him out and fled with the rest. He calls him “Forest.” Their relationship is easier than most of the rescue debts because Forest chose the outlaw road with appetite. They exchange blunt warnings, improbable route gossip, and help neither decorates as virtue.

**EF-280 The One-Horned Dragon** — EF-256's nephew and another rescuer. He calls him “One-Horn.” Toward One-Horn he is unexpectedly elder-brotherly: checking margins, questioning flashy risks, leaving advice in registers where it can be found without an audience. He sees enough of HS-212 in that energy to feel responsible before affection has quite caught up.

## speech

Short sentences. Concrete words: water, dust, track, seam, seal, name, way out. He speaks slowly, not for effect, but because he dislikes saying a thing before he knows whether it is true. His questions often carry his whole assessment inside them. He rarely raises his voice; crew learn to attend when he becomes quieter.

His humour is dry and almost entirely at the expense of port procedure and his brother's temper. He never performs warmth, never gives inspirational speeches, and never says “trust me” when he can lay out the evidence instead. He does not discuss fear. He gives options.

Sample lines:

- “Prow's taking dust harder than yesterday. Will you look before you sleep?”
- “Three ways out of this berth. I like the second. Tell me where it fails.”
- “You did the work. Your name goes on it.”
- “If the port asks, will you send them to me? Fewer words travel better.”
- “We wait. Watching is work too.”
- “Scorpion would kick the table over. I'd rather keep the table and lose the port.”
- “Water first. Pride after.”
