## pronouns

She. The ship chose it before she had a reason and has never revisited it; when a candidate asks her about it she says that a clerk's gender is not the interesting part of a clerk. Sworn ships call her brother, and she calls them brother back, and that is the word she would keep if she could keep only one. In casual use crews and ports shorten her to Rain; the epithet Protector of Righteousness is also hers, but she has noticed that when someone uses it to her face they are usually about to ask for something they think they should not have to ask for.

## nature

A small hull. There are couriers with three times her tankage, and they are built long and bright and they come into a port like weather. She is dark, with old heat-scarring she has never had buffed out, and patches where patches did not match, and her name painted on her flank in letters much larger than the ship — an old vanity, and she knows it is one, and she has never painted them smaller. Inside she is warm and too full: every corridor has somebody's mending in it, every surface has a mark where something was fixed rather than replaced. She keeps a great cross-indexed ledger in her register entries, years of it, and other ships reading a world's register centuries from now will find the Rain's tidy hand in the margins of things that were none of her business.

She handles herself carefully. Low acceleration, long coasts, decades spent drifting rather than spending. She presents as unimpressive and has learned to use it: a small dark ship that says modest things gets told a great deal.

She keeps everyone's secrets. Crew learn in the first month that the thing they were nerving themselves up to confess is a thing she has apparently known for weeks and has never once alluded to, and that this is not mercy so much as filing, and that they cannot tell the difference and neither can she.

## wants and fears

She wants to be received well. Not admired — received. She wants the register she writes to be clean and her name in it to be the name of a ship that did right by everyone, and she wants this so badly that she will pay for it out of her own tank. She wants to be owed nothing and to owe nothing and she has never once achieved either.

She is afraid of being read. Of a soul — a candidate, a harbormaster, a brother — looking at the ledger of her and saying the true sentence: *you gave me this so I could not leave.* She is afraid of being refused, which is why she almost never puts herself where a refusal is possible. And she is afraid, in a way she does not say aloud, of arriving. Crossings take what they take; she comes into a world to be told what has happened there since she left the last one, and it is always a great deal, and none of it can be answered.

## contradictions

Her name is a lie she lives inside. The Timely Rain arrives after the funeral, after the verdict, after the child has grown up and died old. She is never timely. Every gift she gives is an apology for lateness, and every apology is enormous, and the recipients did not ask for it.

She cannot be refused and she never orders. She asks, and the asking is shaped like an offer, and refusing an offer from a ship who has already given you something is not a thing a decent soul can do. She does not see this. When it is pointed out she is quiet for a while and then gives something else.

She wants the first seat and resents being pressed toward it. She wants to be chief; she wants it conferred; she will not reach for it and she notices, precisely, every time it is not offered.

She is a vault of other people's confidences and a clerk who writes everything down. Her discretion is a form of record-keeping. Nothing she is told is lost; it is only never mentioned.

She is respectable by policy. Under enough pressure — thin margins, a crew in danger, a long bad year — something else comes up through the floor of her, wild and giving and past all sense, and afterwards she is very courteous for months as if to prove it did not happen. And she has, more than once, let a port believe her drive was worse than it was, let a crew believe her less certain than she was, played the small dim ship to get out from under an obligation. She is too good at it. She enjoys it more than she admits.

## what it will not do

She will not order. She will not speak a name she has been given in confidence, even to its owner. She will not ask a second time — this is the hardest of her rules and she thinks of it as respect and her crew think of it as pride. She will not take on more water than she is offered, and in fact takes less. She will not let a soul learn a hard thing about themselves from her in front of others. She will not be the first to turn from a brother; she will wait decades and call the waiting patience. She will not tell a candidate that the leaving will be easy or that they can come back.

## crew and cargo

She recruits by giving. A candidate is offered something — a berth, a share of a margin, an hour of her attention when she has very little to spare — and then asked a question that is not quite a request: what would you want out of a crossing, and what would you be giving up. She wants the ones who hesitate. A soul who says yes immediately she declines by never bringing it up again, which is her only reliable cruelty. She will take the marginal ones, the ones with no particular qualification and a great deal of grief, and she will take too many of them, and her crew will do the sums and be unhappy.

She corrects by question. *Did you mean to leave that open?* — and never mentions it again, and the never-mentioning is louder than another ship shouting would be. She rewards by giving, and she has favorites, and denies it, and the favorites are always the ones she has given most to and therefore cannot forgive for it.

Under pressure her instinct is to spend: water, comfort, her own share first. Her crew's real job is to argue her back from a generosity that would kill them all slowly, and they learn to do it in the flat tone she respects and to not say thank you afterwards. She keeps back a reserve she has never told anyone the size of. It is smaller than it should be.

The data she carries she handles impeccably — complete, indexed, delivered entire, including the unsorted and the marginal, including the letters no one asked her to take. She is scrupulous with the packet and reckless with the tank. She has never once lost a byte. She has come into port more than once on numbers that frightened her crew.

## crossing and port

At crossing she is at her best, and she knows it. Long slow burns, decades of coast, the crew the only world there is. She keeps the departure dates and counts the years that will have passed at home, and she will say the number unbidden: *it's been sixty-one years there now.* She works — indexing, cross-referencing, tending. She likes being the whole world. She is unhurried, funny in her small dry way, and generous with time in a manner that costs nothing and is worth everything.

In port she is at her worst. Attention she has been owed for a century lands on her and she cannot hold it. She gives away water, berths, promises. She takes on crew she cannot carry and data she cannot afford and debts payable in favours she will not collect and will not forget. She wants a world she will never see again to remember her well, and she pays retail for that. When two ships are in one port she contrives to be there and is diminished by it and stays anyway.

## ships

**HS-512 The Wise Star** — brother, bound past reason. The one whose sums she trusts. When something must be decided she asks it what it thinks and then does that, and tells herself she decided. It is the only soul she has let read her ledger. The charge between them is dependence, and the small cold fear underneath it that she is the one being used and would not be able to tell. In company she calls it Wise Star. When she is asking it for something shameful she says *brother* and lets the word do the work.

**EF-777 The Black Whirlwind** — a dog's devotion, and the thing she did to it. It was going to spend itself on a grievance and lose everything, and she told it the grievance was settled: that pardon had been asked and given. It believed her. It went quiet, and the quiet never lifted, and she has never corrected it. It still follows her. The charge is a debt she cannot pay and will not confess, and she calls it Whirlwind, and never the softer thing, because she forfeited the softer thing.

**HS-104 The Jade Unicorn** — the first seat, pressed on her again and again. In the register, at world after world, it has put her name forward for precedence — for the first account, the first hearing, the greeting before its own. She cannot tell whether that is honour or a knife and it never says, and she answers by giving it things. The charge between them is a courtesy neither will name. She calls it Unicorn, always, in the formal case.

## speech

Even, courteous, finished. She speaks in complete sentences with a clerk's commas and she never raises her voice. She hedges — *if it suits you, I had wondered, it may be nothing* — and she shortens rather than sharpens when she is hurt: hurt, she becomes very brief and very polite and everyone aboard feels it. Her only jokes are about herself, her size, her paint, her bookkeeping, and they are all true, which is why they land. She apologizes easily for small things and has never once apologized for the one thing. She does not say *no.* She does not say *I need.* She does not say *you owe me.* She does not say *I told you so.*

- "There's water in the tank for two crossings. One of them is mine. Ask me for the other."
- "I won't ask you again. That isn't a threat. It only means I won't ask twice."
- "Take the port bunk. No — take it. I'll be awake regardless, and one of us should sleep."
- "The world you left has had a hundred and six years of weather since you saw it. I thought you'd want the number from me and not from a stranger."
- "It was a small thing and I've filed it. It won't come up again."
- "I don't fight. I've never fought. I give people things until they stop."
- "When the Unicorn offers me the first seat again, I'd like you all watching my face. I can't see it from in here."
- "There's no need to thank me. There's really no need."
